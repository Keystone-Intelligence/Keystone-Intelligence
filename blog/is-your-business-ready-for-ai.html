Is Your Business Ready for AI? A 6-Step Guide for Irish SMEs

Jamie Walsh Updated October 2026 10 min read

Most owners start with the same question: what AI should we use? ChatGPT, Copilot, an agent, a point solution. You can answer that in an afternoon.

The question that decides how far you get is harder. Is this company ready to use AI well? That one is about your work, your data, your knowledge, your people and your rules. It takes months to answer properly, and it matters far more than the choice of tool.

This guide is a plain AI readiness assessment for Irish SMEs. It sets out six steps, with a checklist at the end you can run against your own business.

You already have an AI strategy. Nobody chose it.

Look at any team of ten or more people today and you will find two groups.

Type one

Already using it, invisibly

On personal accounts and their own phones, with company and client information, outside anything you can see, control or learn from.

Type two

Not using it at all

Falling behind quietly, while the same role in another company gets faster every month.

You almost certainly have both, and both are a problem. The first puts client data somewhere you cannot see it, which is a GDPR exposure as much as anything else. The second leaves money on the table.

So you already have an AI strategy. It wrote itself, and nobody chose it. A deliberate one matters because the gap compounds: every month, the work you have made AI-ready gets cheaper and faster, and the work you have not stays exactly as expensive as it is now.

Quick check: five signs you are ready to start

Before the six steps, a fast test. If three or more of these describe your business, you are ready to start with AI.

  1. Your team does the same manual tasks again and again. There is a "that should be automated" list, and it keeps growing.
  2. Slow responses are costing you time or deals. A quote, a referral letter or a tender goes out in days when a competitor's goes out in hours.
  3. Your data is captured but not used. Sales records, case histories and transaction logs sit in systems instead of informing decisions.
  4. Overhead is growing faster than revenue. Every new client brings more admin, not less.
  5. You know your biggest problem but have not solved it. The issue is clear. What a fix would look like, cost and return is not.

Why smaller companies can move faster

It is easy to assume AI favours big companies with big budgets. In practice, smaller businesses have three real advantages.

Less legacy

No 20-year technology stack

You can choose cleaner systems before workarounds become permanent.

Less friction

No 12-month procurement cycle

An owner can test a useful process while a larger competitor is still forming a steering committee.

Less change

No 500-person rollout

Habits are easy to set while there are twelve of you.

The opportunity is to build the business around AI now, rather than recreating the old company and bolting AI on later.

STEP 01 Find the work

Start with the business, not the AI. Look for the places where people repeatedly read, move, check, rewrite or reconcile information. That is where time, money and attention are being wasted.

Ask your team five questions:

  • What do you do repeatedly?
  • What takes too long?
  • Where do mistakes happen?
  • Where are you copying data between systems?
  • Where are expensive people doing low-value work?

Then put a number beside each answer before you think about solutions. Qualifying 50 inbound leads a week at ten minutes each is more than eight hours a week. Numbers like that tell you where to start. Our guide on how to find AI use cases sets out the full method, including a simple opportunity map you can fill in.

STEP 02 Fix the foundations

AI can only work with what it can reach. Before any build, check that the information a task depends on is reachable and that your systems can connect.

What the company knows

Reachable data

  • Core systems, CRM and transactions
  • Documents, inboxes and shared drives
  • Clear ownership and sensible permissions
  • APIs or other ways for systems to connect
How the company works

Documented knowledge

  • SOPs, including the exceptions
  • Policies, examples and decision rules
  • The unwritten "we always do it this way"
  • Voice-note the process and let AI draft it

Garbage in, garbage out. The oldest rule in computing still applies. Whatever you point AI at is what you get back, so AI does not fix a fragmented business. It exposes one.

The good news is that reachable is the bar, not perfect. You do not need to clean every system in the company. You need the data behind your first project to be accessible and sensible. And doing this work improves the business before a single AI system goes live.

STEP 03 Capture the knowledge

In most Irish SMEs, the way the business really runs lives in a few people's heads. How a tricky client is invoiced. Which supplier always needs a follow-up call. What "urgent" actually means in the operations team.

AI cannot follow a process nobody has written down. So write it down: standard operating procedures that include the exceptions, the decision rules people apply without thinking, and the unwritten habits that keep things running.

A practical tip: ask the person who does the task to talk through it on a voice note, then let AI draft the document. Ten minutes of talking becomes a first draft they only need to correct.

Once that knowledge is captured, it stops being a risk when someone is on holiday or leaves. It becomes company infrastructure.

Avoid AI silos

Left alone, every department will adopt its own AI tool. Marketing has already found a writing assistant. Finance is pasting numbers into a chatbot. Nobody told you, and none of it connects. Different tools give different answers to the same question, and whatever anyone learns stays inside their own team.

The answer is one shared company layer that sits under every team, with permissions still in place. Finance sees financial data. Sales sees the CRM. Client and employee records stay restricted. What everybody shares is the foundation underneath: the data, the SOPs, the rules and the audit trail.

Keep that layer model-agnostic. AI models are improving too fast to design your business around one vendor. Treat the model as a replaceable part: use the best one today and swap it next year without rebuilding anything. This is how we approach custom AI development, and it is how you avoid paying for lock-in you never needed.

STEP 04 Bring your people with you

Handing out licences is not enablement. The usual rollout goes like this: buy seats, send an announcement, link a help page. Early adopters improve. Everyone else quietly goes back to the old way.

What makes it stick is teaching people what the AI is actually doing, not which buttons to press. Someone who learns a click path can repeat one task. Someone who understands that the AI is reading, comparing and drafting starts seeing uses for it across their own job.

Here is how that played out at an accounting practice we worked with.

01 · One task

Read the postings

An AI agent reads the text descriptions on accounting postings.

02 · One capability

The penny drops

The team realises that if it can read text, it can classify, flag and recommend.

03 · Many uses

The team finds the next one

The same capability is reused wherever a judgement starts with reading text.

The team brought the next three use cases forward themselves. That is the goal. The same thing happens in healthcare: nobody spends ten years training to do admin, and clinicians who get their paperwork back pull AI in themselves. Our piece on AI for Irish GP practices shows what that looks like day to day.

STEP 05 Start small and measure

One visible win beats a strategy document. Pick a narrow first project, record how long the task takes today, and measure it again once the new system is live.

The gains do not need to look dramatic task by task. Small savings, repeated every week by every person, add up.

1 hour saved per person, per week
× 52 weeks in a year
52 hours more than a full working week of capacity, per person

Across a team of ten, that is 520 hours a year, from one hour a week each. Our breakdown of what AI costs an Irish business shows how to turn savings like these into a payback figure.

A small win is also change management. It gives people a reason to believe the tools are useful. As they see what AI can do, they get curious and start spotting the next opportunity themselves.

STEP 06 Set the rules

Most businesses end up at one of two extremes by default. Everything is allowed because nothing was ever said, or everything is banned because saying no felt safer. Four written answers are enough to avoid both.

01 · SEE

What can AI see?

Which data, documents and systems are in bounds, and what never leaves the approved setup.

02 · DO

What can AI do?

Draft? Update records? Send messages? Move money? Make the permitted actions explicit.

03 · APPROVE

What needs a human?

Match review to the cost of being wrong. Client-facing or financially material work gets checked.

04 · OWN

Who is accountable?

Every AI process needs a named person who owns it when something goes wrong.

This is commercial, not just compliance. Without rules, you cannot let AI near anything that matters, so it stays on low-value work and never pays for itself. Our guide to writing an AI policy for an Irish SME turns these four questions into a one-page template.

Your AI readiness checklist

Run your business against these six points. Anything you cannot tick is where to start.

AI readiness checklist
  • We know where the work is. We have asked the team the five questions and put an hours figure beside the top answers.
  • The data is reachable. The systems, documents and inboxes behind our first project can be accessed, with sensible permissions.
  • The knowledge is written down. The process for our first project is documented, including the exceptions.
  • Our people understand what AI does. The team using it knows what it is reading, comparing and drafting, not just which buttons to press.
  • We have one small, measured first project. It is owned by one team, has a baseline, and is safe if it gets something wrong.
  • The rules are written down. We have answered what AI can see, what it can do, what needs a human and who owns it.

Common questions about AI readiness

What is an AI readiness assessment?

A structured look at whether a business can use AI well. It covers which work is worth changing, whether the data can be reached, whether processes are written down, whether staff understand what AI does, and whether there are clear rules. The output is a short list of first projects, each with a cost and an expected return. Our Discovery Report does exactly this for €1,000, delivered within 48 hours and credited against the build if you go ahead.

How long does it take to get ready for AI?

Choosing a tool takes an afternoon. Getting the company ready takes months. You do not need to finish before you start, though. A narrow first project can be agreed in two weeks and show a result within two months, and it will tell you what to fix next.

Does our data need to be perfect?

No. It needs to be reachable. Tidying the data behind your first project is part of the work, but you do not need to clean the whole business first.

Do we need an AI policy before we start?

No law in Ireland requires a written AI policy. GDPR still applies whenever AI touches personal data, and the EU AI Act asks businesses that use AI to take steps to build AI literacy among their staff. A one-page policy covers both. Our AI policy guide includes a template.

Is my business too small for AI?

Usually the opposite. Less legacy, shorter decision chains and fewer people to bring along mean smaller companies can move faster. The first project is sized to the business, and a fixed-price build starts at €1,000.

Prepare the company. Then choose the AI.

Almost everybody will have access to the same AI models. The advantage will come from what you build around them: your work, data, knowledge, people and rules. Build that layer well and the models can change without the company having to.
